50_DECISION_HISTORY_AND_ENGINEERING_EVOLUTION

1. Purpose of Decision History and Engineering Evolution

The Decision History and Engineering Evolution document preserves the record of why specific architectural and operational paths were chosen. It serves as a technical audit trail for grant reviewers, engineers, and stakeholders, ensuring that institutional memory persists as the project scales.

2. Institutional Memory Philosophy

The project prioritizes the preservation of context. Understanding why a design was selected—and why alternatives were rejected—is considered a primary engineering asset. This philosophy values:

Contextual Continuity: Ensuring that new personnel understand the constraints that shaped current systems.

Avoiding Cyclical Errors: Preventing the re-exploration of rejected concepts without new data.

Hypothesis Tracking: Documenting the progression from initial assumption to verified operational fact.

3. Early Conceptual Foundations

Initial conceptualization focused on the necessity of removing sargassum from the marine environment before it reached the beach. Early frameworks emphasized environmental remediation and the capture of biogas and chemicals as a means of offsetting operational costs.

4. Evolution from Vessel-Centric to Modular Processing

A critical pivot occurred early in the project’s development, moving away from a single, centralized vessel-based processing facility toward a distributed modular architecture.

Reasoning: Centralized processing created significant logistical bottlenecks and increased the risk of total system failure if the primary vessel required maintenance.

Outcome: The adoption of modular digestion chambers and floating tile barges allowed for parallel processing and decoupled the harvest cycle from the refinement cycle.

5. Development of the Tile Barge Philosophy

The tile barge system evolved from a simple harvesting aid into a multi-functional operational platform.

Iterative Shift: Initial designs considered heavy industrial barges, which were rejected in favor of low-profile, modular "tiles."

Functional Integration: The realization that drainage and marine-life separation must occur immediately at the point of harvest led to the current grated-surface design and "low-lift" workflow philosophy.

6. Evolution of the Digestion Chamber Architecture

The digestion chamber architecture shifted from fixed vertical tanks to horizontal, modular HDPE drums.

Modularity Driver: The need to move biomass through different environments (offshore to shoreside) without reloading necessitated a containerized batch unit.

Mechanical Simplification: Selecting horizontal HDPE drums facilitated lower-energy manual agitation and simplified the integration of quick-connect tooling interfaces.

7. Containment-First Operational Evolution

Early workflows prioritized processing speed, but this was refined into a "containment-first" hierarchy.

Safety Pivot: The potential for reactive gas accumulation (hydrogen/methane/chlorine) required that all fluid and gas transfers be treated as high-priority containment events.

Workflow Integration: Containment protocols became the primary driver for subsystem interface design (e.g., dry-break couplings and sealed hatches).

8. Maintenance-First Philosophy Development

The harsh Belizean marine environment forced an evolution in maintenance thinking.

Realization: High-complexity automated systems were deemed unserviceable in nearshore splash zones.

Philosophical Shift: The project formally adopted a hierarchy where maintenance integrity takes priority over operational throughput, favoring rugged, simple mechanical interfaces.

9. Complexity Reduction and Survivability Lessons

Repeated engineering refinement demonstrated that operational survivability in Belizean marine conditions depends heavily on reducing unnecessary subsystem complexity.

Operational Drivers: Salt exposure, workforce fatigue, maintenance burden, handling difficulty, and field-service limitations consistently favored simpler modular systems over highly optimized but fragile architectures.

Strategic Intent: The project evolved toward mechanical simplicity not as a rejection of technology, but as a response to real-world operational survivability requirements.

10. Workforce and Operational Simplification Evolution

Refining the interaction between personnel and hardware led to the rejection of complex digital controls in favor of tactile mechanical systems.

Localization Strategy: To support a localized Belizean workforce, systems were redesigned for manual proficiency (cranks, bellows, manual valves) to ensure field-serviceability and operational independence from high-tech supply chains.

11. PMA and Auxiliary Energy Philosophy Evolution

The role of manual and PMA generation moved from a "backup" concept to a "baseline redundancy" layer.

Integration History: Initial power models relied heavily on solar; however, the need for 24/7 monitoring and emergency recovery led to the formal integration of PMA-assisted charging for batteries and shared capacitor banks.

Power Architecture Clarification: The power architecture was later clarified to distinguish vessel electric propulsion and solar/battery charging from capacitor-buffered electrolysis delivery. The PSV is intended to use battery-supported electric propulsion, with solar energy charging the vessel battery system. PMA/manual generation remains a support charging pathway for vessel batteries and also serves as a dedicated charging input for electrolysis capacitor banks. Electrolysis should be described as Capacitor-Buffered Electrolysis Delivery, where stored energy is discharged through controlled power electronics, isolation, and discharge-control hardware into the selected hydroxide or sulfur recovery workflow. This clarification does not validate propulsion range, motor sizing, battery capacity, PMA output capacity, gear ratio, flywheel mass, capacitor sizing, electrolysis throughput, duty cycle, or continuous human-powered electrolysis capability.

12. Electrolysis Workflow Clarification History

Electrolysis was initially a generalized recovery concept but was later clarified into two distinct, isolated workflows (hydroxide and sulfur).

Refinement Reason: Preventing cross-contamination and managing the specific gas byproducts of different recovery targets required a formal separation of the electrolytic cells and fluid pathways on the Support Vessel.

13. Gas Handling and Containment Evolution

Gas management transitioned from a byproduct concern to a core containment architecture.

Architectural Change: The decision was made to treat the digestion chambers as generation/transfer units only, moving long-term gas storage to dedicated external containment modules to reduce the safety risks associated with pressurized processing vessels.

14. Environmental Stewardship Integration History

Stewardship moved from a compliance goal to an integrated workflow component.

Operational Integration: The "low-impact" philosophy replaced "leave no trace" to better reflect the industrial reality of marine remediation. Procedures like marine-life separation were moved to the start of the workflow (on the tiles) to maximize organism survival rates.

15. Economic Philosophy Evolution

Economic modeling shifted from "export-driven revenue" to a "Belize-First" industrial resilience model.

Strategic Pivot: Focus moved toward localized import substitution (recovering chemicals for local industrial use) to increase project stability and support Belizean economic self-sufficiency.

16. Rejected Concepts and Lessons Learned

Automated Conveyors: Rejected for offshore use due to salt-spray corrosion and mechanical complexity.

High-Pressure Digestion: Rejected in favor of low-pressure containment to reduce material costs and improve safety margins.

Vertical Agitation: Rejected in modular chambers as it complicated horizontal transport and loading/unloading workflows.

17. Operational Unknown Preservation Philosophy

The project formally adopted a "Documentation Integrity Rule" to ensure that gaps in knowledge (unresolved variables) are not masked by speculative assumptions. This preserves the honesty of the technical record for future engineering cycles.

18. Data-Driven Refinement Philosophy

Engineering evolution is tied directly to the 32_Series validation doctrine. Every architectural pivot must be supported by recorded operational evidence or mechanical performance data logged during field tests.

19. Long-Term Repository Evolution Philosophy

The Grant repository is designed as a living document. It is expected to expand only as physical subsystems diverge or as operational validation requires architectural separation, maintaining a strict lock on top-level categories to prevent scope creep.

20. Known Constraints

Historical Knowledge Gaps: Early conceptual data may be less precise than current operational logs.

Environmental Variability: Historical conclusions based on specific Belizean seasons may require re-validation during anomalous weather events.

51_TERMINOLOGY_AND_STANDARDIZATION

1. Purpose of Terminology and Standardization

The Terminology and Standardization document establishes the official vocabulary and naming conventions for the sargassum processing project. Its primary goal is to prevent semantic drift, ensure clarity in grant applications, and maintain technical consistency across all repository files.

2. Repository Language Philosophy

The repository utilizes Technical Plain Language. All terminology must be descriptive, objective, and grounded in physical reality. Speculative, metaphorical, or marketing-oriented phrasing is strictly prohibited to ensure the repository remains a high-fidelity technical record.

3. Official System Naming Conventions

The Project: Referred to as the "Belize-based sargassum interception and processing system" or "the project."

The Repository: Referred to as "Grant" or "the repository."

Subsystems: Must be named according to their numerical series designation (e.g., "Modular Tile Barge System," "Modular Digestion Chambers").

PSV: The standard abbreviation for the Primary Support Vessel.

Mothership Role: Descriptive term for the PSV's function as the central logistical and transport hub.

Deck Staging: The organized placement of modular units on the vessel surface.

Electric Propulsion System: The intended PSV propulsion architecture using battery-supported electric drive systems. Exact motor type, battery capacity, range, charging schedule, and propulsion duty cycle remain [UNRESOLVED ENGINEERING VARIABLE] or [PENDING VALIDATION] until supported by physical data.

5. Modular Tile Barge Terminology

Tile: A single, discrete modular floating unit.

Array: A collection of tiles interconnected for operation.

Low-Lift: The ergonomic philosophy of minimizing vertical movement of saturated biomass.

Grated Surface: The specific dewatering and work interface of the tile.

6. Digestion Chamber Terminology

Chamber: The individual HDPE drum processing unit.

Batch: A single load of biomass undergoing a discrete processing cycle.

Horizontal Orientation: The sideways position of the chamber during agitation.

Tooling Interface: The mechanical quick-connect system for internal components.

7. Electrolysis Workflow Terminology

Refinement: The conversion of effluents into recovered chemical targets.

Hydroxide Processing: The specific workflow for isolating hydroxides.

Sulfur Recovery: The specific workflow for isolating sulfur-related compounds.

Separation: The physical or electrochemical isolation of target materials.

Capacitor-Buffered Electrolysis Delivery: The controlled power-delivery method in which energy stored in a dedicated capacitor bank is discharged into selected electrolysis workflows through isolation and discharge-control hardware. This term describes power buffering and delivery, not [VERIFIED] throughput, energy sufficiency, continuous-duty performance, or electrolysis efficiency.

8. Gas Handling and Containment Terminology

Containment: The physical isolation of gases or fluids within a defined, leak-proof boundary.

Scrubbing: The chemical neutralization or purification of a gas stream.

Low-Pressure: Operational states near atmospheric pressure (avoiding "high-pressure" unless validated).

Headspace: The gas-filled volume above the liquid/solid biomass inside a chamber.

9. PMA and Auxiliary Energy Terminology

Auxiliary: Secondary or supportive systems that are not the primary operational driver.

Manual Fallback: The capability to operate mechanical systems by hand during power loss.

PMA-Assisted: Systems utilizing the Permanent Magnet Alternator for supplemental power.

PMA Flywheel Manual Generator: A human-powered PMA charging assembly using a handcrank, geared drive, and flywheel smoothing to charge vessel batteries or electrolysis capacitor banks. Exact gear ratio, flywheel mass, watt-hour yield, and operator duty cycle remain [UNRESOLVED ENGINEERING VARIABLE] or [PENDING VALIDATION] until measured.

Redundancy: The provision of secondary systems to ensure critical safety functions.

10. Maintenance and Inspection Terminology

Maintenance Integrity: The state of a subsystem being fully serviced and safe for operation.

Cycle Count: The recorded number of times a component (e.g., a seal or hatch) has been operated.

Tactile Verification: Hand-checking seals, linkages, or ports for physical integrity.

11. Environmental Stewardship Terminology

Remediation: The active reversal or mitigation of environmental damage (sargassum influx).

Low-Impact: Operations designed to minimize secondary ecological disturbance.

Bycatch: Non-target marine life captured within harvested biomass.

Chain-of-Custody: The traceable record of concentrated material movement to prevent release.

12. Workforce and Operational Doctrine Terminology

Technical Discipline: Strict adherence to established workflows and maintenance protocols.

Hardware Empathy: An operational culture valuing the preservation and careful handling of equipment.

Handoff: The formal transfer of system status information between shifts.

13. Validation and Uncertainty Classification Standards

All unverified data must use the following official tags:

[VERIFIED]: Confirmed through physical data or testing.

[ASSUMPTION]: Logic based on probable conditions.

[HYPOTHESIS]: A testable prediction.

[ESTIMATED BEHAVIOR]: Predicted mechanical/chemical outcomes.

[PENDING VALIDATION]: Established designs awaiting testing.

[UNRESOLVED ENGINEERING VARIABLE]: Identified gaps in specifications.

14. Emergency and Safe-State Terminology

Safe-State: A stabilized, low-risk configuration where containment is prioritized.

Hold-State: A controlled operational pause where systems remain ready for restart.

Shutdown: Total cessation of activity and power to a subsystem.

Stabilization: The act of returning a malfunctioning system to a predictable, non-chaotic state.

15. Material and Corrosion Terminology

Embrittlement: The loss of ductility in polymers (HDPE) due to UV or chemical exposure.

Splash Zone: Areas subject to repeated wetting and drying cycles of saltwater.

Sacrificial: Components intended to wear or corrode to protect primary structures.

16. Data Collection and Logging Terminology

Anomaly: Any deviation from expected mechanical or chemical behavior.

Irregularity: A minor, non-interruptive deviation that must still be recorded.

Yield: The measurable quantity of gas or chemical recovered from a batch.

17. Prohibited Terminology and Misleading Phrasing

To prevent repository drift, the following terms/concepts are prohibited:

Perpetual Energy / Closed-Loop Energy: Implies violation of thermodynamics.

Zero-Waste: An unvalidated industrial ideal; use "Residual Management" instead.

Industrial Scale: Prohibited until specific volumetric throughput is [VERIFIED].

Guaranteed Outcomes: All ecological or economic benefits must be phrased as "targets" or "projections" until validated.

Fully Autonomous / Self-Operating: Prohibited unless specific subsystem automation has been physically validated. Use "manual-assisted," "semi-manual," "operator-controlled," or "low-complexity mechanical workflow" where appropriate.

18. Repository Consistency and Update Rules

Update Trigger: If a new operational term is required, it must be added to this document before being utilized in subsystem files.
