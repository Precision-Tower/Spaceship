3. Current Boat Ruling

The core architectural directive of this vessel is preserved as follows:

The boat is a clean, inspectable, ballast-testable support raft, not a processing vessel.

The physical system must be optimized for visual inspectability, rapid leak detection, clear structural access, and clean/dirty separation. It does not carry raw, uncontained sargassum biomass, nor does it host biological digestion systems directly on its main deck structure unless explicitly governed by validated interface and containment logic.

7. System Role

To keep our system boundary clear, we have defined what the boat is and what it is not.

The Boat IS:

A clean support vessel providing clean-side working space, subject to physical testing.

A control spine routing dry power, network, and communication lines.

A process control support platform hosting logic controllers, telemetry, and logging arrays.

An electrolysis support platform candidate for hosting isolated electrical and control components, subject to physical testing and subsystem validation.

A monitoring/logging platform capturing sensor telemetry from the environment and adjacent systems.

An emergency shutdown/isolation support node providing physical levers, valves, and power-kill switches.

A clean interface manager regulating transfer pipes and umbilical lines to the dirty barge.

The Boat IS NOT:

A dirty sargassum processing surface.

A biomass drainage deck or sorting station.

A bio-digestion platform or raw anaerobic reactor deck.

A finalized operational vessel ready for unescorted deployment.

8. Clean / Dirty Boundary

The structural, fluidic, and operational boundary between the support raft and the adjacent processing environment must be strictly managed:

Dirty Side Location: All raw sargassum biomass handling, sorting, washing, mechanical dewatering, biological digestion, and effluent drainage remain completely isolated on the barge/tile side.

Clean Side Location: The boat serves as the clean zone. It contains dry controls, safety instrumentation, electrical generation, human monitoring stations, and emergency system isolates.

Boundary Interfaces: Any connection crossing this boundary (electrical, data, hydraulic, or material transfer) must pass through defined, sacrificial interface modules. If a spill, leak, or mechanical failure occurs at the interface, it is intended to localize damage or leakage at replaceable, inspectable boundary components while preserving containment and preventing uncontrolled release. This remains [PENDING VALIDATION].

9. Accepted Architecture Direction

Our modeling assumes the following structural layout components:

HDPE Drum-Row Flotation Grid: Buoyancy provided by longitudinal rows of HDPE drum candidates or other validated flotation units.

Drum Capture/Restraint Frame: A mechanical containment structure designed to trap, align, and prevent shifting of flotation units under cyclic loading.

PVC Distributed Short-Span Truss Chassis: A lightweight, non-corrosive, distributed truss network built using a PVC distributed short-span truss candidate, subject to material and joint validation.

Minimum Viable Inspection Space: Sufficient inspection access to preserve visibility of flotation, restraints, joints, and leak-sensitive areas, subject to mockup testing.

Raised Removable Deck Panels: Removable access panels to allow top-down access to any portion of the underlying truss chassis or flotation grid.

Central Clean Operator Aisle: A clear continuous centerline pathway, width [UNRESOLVED ENGINEERING VARIABLE], kept entirely free of process equipment, piping, or high-voltage lines.

Side Utility/Equipment Bays: Symmetrical structural bays flanking the central aisle designed to host skid-mounted equipment, containment tubs, or battery arrays.

Reinforced Removable Skid Zones: Candidate bay locations intended to distribute modular equipment loads into candidate structural members, subject to deflection and point-load testing.

Sacrificial Clean/Dirty Side Interface Modules: Easily swappable connection zones at the deck edge intended to localize dirty-side contact, hose strain, splash exposure, and potential leakage at replaceable boundary components while preserving containment.

Dependency Logic

Start with space and reach because bad access geometry is cheapest to fix before materials exist.

Then test drum restraint because the raft depends on flotation units staying captured and inspectable.

Then test deck, truss, and skid bays because these define the repeatable structural language.

Then test interface and tow sections because those are boundary loads with high damage potential.

Only then run the base raft float/ballast test, because it should use the best current restraint and frame logic.

Appendix Status: [NON-CANONICAL MOCKUP PROTOCOL — PENDING VALIDATION]

1. Mockup Purpose

This appendix defines the first low-cost physical layout interrogation for the Clean Support Raft / Boat Prototype. It tests degraded_reach and mockup_spacing using tape, cardboard, string/rope, markers, labels, and mock equipment placeholders before structural materials are committed.

2. Materials Needed

Layout Tape: Minimum 200 ft of highly visible painter's tape or flagging tape.

Vertical Mockups: Heavy cardboard boxes, mailing tubes, or folded cardboard sheets to represent vertical equipment bounds.

Boundary Line: Colored string or thin nylon rope (minimum 100 ft) to model physical handrails or frame edges.

Marking Tools: Thick permanent markers, adhesive labels, and warning tape for color-coding utility boundaries.

Measurement Tools: Tape measure or folding ruler (strictly for recording relative mockup layout coordinates, not establishing production design parameters).

Simulation Hardware: Mock hand tools, mock valve handles, and cardboard tablets to simulate active operator tasks.

3. Layout Elements to Mark

Electrolysis skid candidate footprint.

Monitoring/logging station candidate footprint.

4. Test Actions

Action 01: Baseline Aisle Clearance Transit

An operator walks the taped centerline path while carrying simulated hand tools to evaluate side-to-side clearance.

Action 02: Emergency Controls Degraded Reach Simulation

An operator stands at defined points in the centerline aisle, simulating degraded-access conditions (e.g., knee down, leaning to recover balance), and attempts to reach the mapped locations of primary shutdown switches, disconnects, and isolation valves.

Action 03: Service & Tool Swing Test

Position an operator next to the cardboard skid mockups. Have them simulate maintenance actions (e.g., wrenching, valve turning, filter swapping) to observe elbow room and tool path clearance relative to adjacent equipment walls.

Action 04: Simulated Multi-Operator Interference

Two operators simulate task-coordination behaviors in the aisle and bay spaces. Observe if two people cannot maneuver without obstruction or contact conflicts where assistance is being simulated.

Action 05: Deck Access Grating Lift Observation

An operator attempts to simulate grabbing, lifting, and setting aside a marked removable deck panel candidate from the central pathway without stepping outside the aisle footprint.

5. Observations to Record

Visual Contact Points: Note any physical contact between operator elbows/shoulders and adjacent cardboard mockups during transit or simulated maintenance.

Reach Interferences: Document any physical strain, loss of balance, or obstructions preventing a direct hand path to emergency controls.

Tool Blockages: Identify if the swing path of simulated wrenches or maintenance tools is restricted by neighboring mock equipment skids.

Line-of-Sight Blockages: Note if cardboard equipment stacks obstruct the operator's visual line of sight to primary monitoring interfaces or leak-inspection zones.

6. Failure Signs

Cardboard mockups are physically bumped, displaced, or knocked over during normal aisle transit.

An operator is unable to complete a direct hand path to primary emergency controls without losing balance or stepping outside the clean aisle.

Simulated tool swing ranges intersect with adjacent mock equipment profiles.

Two people cannot maneuver without obstruction or contact conflicts where assistance is being simulated.

Removal and lifting maneuvers for simulated deck panels require stepping onto unverified, non-grated, or adjacent equipment zones.

7. Records to Capture

Clearance Coordinates: Record observed relative distance margins between the aisle line and mock skid walls.

Degraded Reach Margins: Log the estimated distance shortfalls where hand access to emergency switches is strained.

Conflict Identifiers: Map and list specific zones where physical collisions occurred during multi-operator simulations.

Appendix E — Mockup 1 Field Run Plan

Appendix Status: [NON-CANONICAL FIELD RUN PLAN — PENDING VALIDATION]

Purpose

This appendix defines the first physical interrogation sequence for Mockup 1: Tape/Cardboard Full-Scale Access Layout. It details the setup order, photo discipline, test run order, blocking issue criteria, floor revision marking rules, and the raw dataset to bring back to the Interactive Repository (Grant/Dinesh) after the field run is completed.

1. What To Set Up First

Execute the setup steps in this strict sequence:

Chassis Bounding Line: Sweep the floor clean of debris. Measure and lay down tape to define the outer bounds of the 24 ft x 12 ft working footprint.

Central Clean Operator Aisle: Apply tape lines to trace the candidate centerline aisle along the entire longitudinal axis. Ensure the pathway is marked clearly and kept empty of all materials.

Bay Grid Subdivisions: Section off the Port and Starboard equipment bays on either side of the aisle using contrasting colored tape.

Mock Skid Positioning: Assemble and place the cardboard boxes representing the battery control, electrolysis, and monitoring skids. Weigh down the boxes with scrap blocks so they do not shift prematurely.

Emergency Interfaces: Place mock emergency stop switches and isolation valve fixtures on vertical cardboard stands at their draft coordinate locations.

Clean/Dirty Boundary: Run colored string or thin rope along the outer starboard edge to model the physical handrail transition toward the simulated dirty barge edge.

2. What To Photograph Before Testing

To preserve the baseline, take these photos before performing any simulated operations:

High-Angle Overall View: A wide-angle photo of the entire 24 ft x 12 ft grid from a ladder or elevated point to capture the layout topology.

Aisle Visual Alignment: A straight-line photo taken from the bow looking toward the stern down the center of the operator aisle.

Equipment Closeness Profile: Close-up photos of the spacing clearances between adjacent cardboard equipment skids.

Emergency Reach Lines: Clear photos showing the line-of-sight from the center aisle to each emergency control switch and isolation valve marker.

3. Order To Perform Test Actions

Conduct the test sequence in the following order:

Unloaded Transit Walk: Walk the centerline aisle back and forth to establish baseline body clearances and locate any natural walking path interferences.

Tool-Carry Simulation: Walk the path while carrying typical field tools (wrenches, toolboxes) to observe if bulkier profiles alter movement lanes.

Static Reach Interrogations: Stand at each emergency reach station. Simulate degraded postures (e.g., knee down, leaning away) and reach for the mock switches, recording any balance strain.

Active Service Clashes: Simulate basic equipment maintenance tasks (e.g., wrench swing, filter replacement, panel lift) at each skid bay, checking for physical boundary conflicts.

Multi-Person Transit Coordination: Run simulated two-person passing scenarios in the aisle and assess if assistance tasks create elbow-room or spatial-overlap interferences.

Panel Removal Lift: Simulate lifting a removable deck panel candidate from the sub-frame structure, observing if the panel's physical size forces the operator to step off the central pathway.

4. Observations That Matter Most

While executing the run plan, watch for and record these specific indicators:

Foot-Fault Frequencies: Count how many times an operator’s foot touches or crosses the taped boundary lines of the central clean aisle.

Shoulder/Elbow Contact: Note whenever an operator’s clothing, body, or simulated tools brush against the cardboard equipment profiles.

Line-of-Sight Blockages: Check if vertical cardboard skids obscure direct line of sight to process hoses, the clean/dirty interface, or adjacent utility bays from the main aisle.

Manual Handling Path Bind: Observe any body twisting, stumbling, or awkward footwork required to lift and set aside the mock deck panels.

5. What Counts As A Blocking Layout Issue

Any of the following occurrences constitutes a layout collision that must be flagged for revision:

An operator must step outside the central clean pathway to reach or actuate an emergency shutdown control.

Simulated maintenance tasks or tool swings require intersecting with neighboring equipment skids or routing paths.

Normal two-person passing maneuvers cannot occur without brushing against equipment skids or stepping over utility bay boundaries.

Lifting and setting aside a removable deck panel candidate is physically blocked by vertical equipment heights or adjacent structures.

Hose or umbilical line paths cross directly over the central operator lane, creating a permanent tripping hazard.

6. How To Mark Revisions On The Floor

If a blocking issue is identified, do not tear up the baseline tape layout. Follow these steps:

Leave the original taped line or mock equipment box in its initial baseline position.

Use a contrasting colored tape (e.g., high-visibility orange or striped warning tape) to outline the proposed new boundary or skid shift.

Label the new line clearly with the Revision ID (e.g., "REV-1B-AISLE") and date.

Photograph the physical offset showing the old line and the new proposed candidate line side-by-side.

Record the offset measurement coordinates in Appendix D before shifting the physical cardboard mockup to its new position.

7. What Information To Bring Back To Grant Afterward

Provide the following formatted dataset to the Interactive Repository (Grant/Dinesh) to update our system model:

The Completed Appendix D Log Matrix: A clean, transcribed CSV-compatible text record of all logged occurrences, classified by observation categories.

The Baseline and Revision Photo Set: Organized folders containing pre-run, in-test, and post-revision photos matched to specific Log IDs.

The Offset Coordinate Map: The physical distance adjustments (X and Y offsets) made to the tape lines or skid positions during floor revisions.

Aisle Width Recommendation: A recommended candidate range for the minimum acceptable centerline aisle width based on tool-carry and passing simulations.

8. What Should Remain Unresolved After The First Run

This test is strictly a spatial access check. The following parameters must remain marked as [UNRESOLVED ENGINEERING VARIABLE] and are not validated by this test:

Actual structural frame flexure or joint twisting under live payload weights.

Seaworthiness, stability, rolling response, or flotation performance on water.

Operating envelope limits under environmental wave, wind, or current loads.

The actual effectiveness or sealing of clean/dirty boundary containment interfaces.

Validated workflow safety or operational approval of equipment configurations.
